TL;DR

When a language model decodes token \(t\), every layer of token \(t-1\) has already finished. Self-attention still connects positions only at the same depth, so a shallow layer of the new token never sees the deeper states of the previous one. Attention Latent Feedback (ALF) lets every sublayer attend to the outputs of the previous token from its own depth upward and mix them into its input. It trains in two parallel passes, decodes in one pass per token, and lowers held-out loss at every scale we train, ahead of feeding back only the final state.

Abstract

During autoregressive decoding, the deeper representations of the previous token have already been computed, yet shallow layers at the current token cannot read them directly because standard self-attention connects positions only at the same depth. We introduce Attention Latent Feedback (ALF), an architecture that allows every sublayer to attend to the outputs of the deeper layers at the previous position. Training unrolls this cross-position recurrence into two parallel passes, whereas decoding remains single-pass using cached previous-token states. Across mixture-of-experts models with up to 4.0B parameters, ALF lowers held-out loss by 0.022–0.024 nats over the standard residual with about 1.1% decoding FLOP overhead, and consistently outperforms prior final-state feedback across all three scales on both standard and attention residual backbones. On MoE-1.6B, ALF matches a 1.38× larger baseline token budget, and on MoE-4.0B it lifts average accuracy across seventeen downstream benchmarks from 45.1% to 47.0%. Analysis further reveals that intermediate and nearby deeper layers drive most of this benefit, allowing a fixed nearest-window variant to retain 89%–94% of the gain at linear read cost while learned attention widens its margin with scale.

Why read more than the final state

Earlier latent feedback hands the next token a single vector: the final state. That state is trained to produce the next-token prediction, and the next position already receives that token as an embedding, so it can expose little of the computation underneath.

To test this, we swept the source block of an additive input feedback path on MoE-1.6B and MoE-2.6B, holding the backbone, parameter count, FLOPs, checkpoint and data order fixed. In both models the best source is a deep intermediate block, block 7 of 8 and block 8 of 10, which lowers held-out loss by 0.0044 and 0.0041 nats relative to the final block. The shallowest sources are far worse, 0.0125 and 0.0067 nats above it.

Intermediate layers therefore carry signal that final-state feedback leaves unused, and the useful depth shifts with the model. ALF removes the need to pick one source at all: every sublayer attends over the whole depth of the previous token and weights it per token and per depth, so the useful layers are discovered during training rather than chosen in advance (Finding 4).

How ALF works

Each sublayer \(l\) draws on two streams. The current stream \(x_{t,l}\) is the input that the backbone already gives it, and the sublayer feedback carries the outputs of position \(t-1\) at depths \(i\ge l\), which position \(t\) has not computed yet.

\[h_{t,l}=(1-\rho_l)\,\textcolor{#3e6ca8}{x_{t,l}}+\rho_l\,c_{t,l}\sum_{i=l}^{2L}\textcolor{#a8672a}{\beta_{t,i\to l}}\,M_i\,\textcolor{#a8672a}{v_{t-1,i}}, \qquad x_{t,1}=e_t+W_E\,\mathrm{RMSNorm}(\textcolor{#a8672a}{z_{t-1}})\]

The mixed input \(h_{t,l}\) replaces \(x_{t,l}\) as the input of sublayer \(l\). The second equation adds the projected final state of token \(t-1\) to the token embedding.

Sublayer feedback

Attention over depth

Every output \(v_{t-1,i}\) of the previous position passes once through a low-rank map \(M_i=I+U_iD_i\) of rank 64. A learned query per sublayer scores the mapped outputs with a softmax over depth (\(\beta\)), and a learned share \(\rho_l\) mixes the result into the current stream.

Input injection

The final state, at the embedding

Following Recycler, a learned projection \(W_E\) of the previous final state \(z_{t-1}\) is added to the token embedding. It carries the accumulated representation that produced the previous prediction, which the individual sublayer outputs do not.

Training and decoding

Two passes in training, one in decoding

Training runs a plain pass and then a feedback pass that reads the first pass shifted by one position, both in parallel over the sequence, during the last 25% of steps. Decoding caches the \(2L\) outputs of the previous token, so each new token takes a single pass.

Five findings

Mixture-of-experts models at three scales (MoE-1.6B, MoE-2.6B and MoE-4.0B) trained under the Marin recipe. FBT and ALF branch from the same checkpoint at 75% of training and see the same data with the same two-pass schedule.

Finding 1

ALF lowers loss at every scale and stays ahead of final-state feedback

On the standard residual, ALF lowers held-out loss by 0.024 nats on MoE-1.6B, and the gain holds at 0.023 and 0.022 nats on the two larger models. FBT also improves on the backbone, yet ALF stays at least 0.008 nats below it at every scale, and on MoE-1.6B it leads FBT in all six validation domains, from code and mathematics to web text.

The loss gains carry over to downstream tasks. On MoE-4.0B, ALF raises the average accuracy over seventeen tasks from 45.1% to 47.0% and leads FBT in all four domains, with the largest gains in mathematics and reading comprehension.

Finding 2

The gain carries over to attention residuals, and the sublayer feedback does most of the work

Attention Residuals (AttnRes) already let each layer weight the earlier layers of its own position. ALF still lowers their loss by margins close to those on the standard residual and widens its lead over FBT to at least 0.012 nats. On MoE-4.0B, it raises AttnRes's downstream accuracy by 1.7 points.

Testing each path alone on MoE-1.6B explains why. The sublayer feedback gives nearly the same gain on both backbones and keeps 96% of ALF's gain on AttnRes, whereas the input injection alone keeps 49% there. Once a backbone links every layer to its token input, reads at every sublayer carry almost all of the remaining benefit.

Finding 3

At a fixed model size, ALF reaches the same loss with fewer tokens and less compute

We trained the standard residual on MoE-1.6B at several token budgets and fitted a power law to its loss. To match ALF, it needs 1.38× as many training tokens, so ALF saves 27% of the data. The second training pass over the last quarter raises ALF's training FLOPs to 1.25× the backbone, which the token savings more than cover: at equal loss, ALF still needs 1.10× less compute.

Across the three sizes, FBT needs 1.07× the training FLOPs of ALF to reach each ALF model's loss. When model size and unique tokens can both grow freely, spending the extra 25% on a larger single-pass backbone is comparably efficient, so ALF pays off most where tokens, model size or inference cost are the binding constraint.

Finding 4

Sublayers read the nearby deeper layers of the previous token

The learned depth weights form the same narrow diagonal band at all three sizes: most of the weight falls on a sublayer's own depth and the next three deeper sublayers of the previous position. On MoE-4.0B, readers in the lower half put 31% of their weight on the two nearest eligible outputs, where a uniform read would put 11%. Interventions on the trained model agree. Keeping only the most useful middle block retains 40% of the sublayer feedback's contribution, while the top quarter of blocks together retains 21%. Intermediate layers of the previous token carry the strongest signal, and the final state alone leaves it out.

Why read more than the final state asks the matching question for a single injected vector: which one block to read when only one is read. Both point at intermediate depths.

Finding 5

A fixed window of four nearby outputs keeps most of the gain at linear read cost

The diagonal band suggests a cheaper variant. We replace the softmax over depth with an equal-weight average of each sublayer's own output and the next three deeper ones, keeping the maps, shares and input injection. This removes every depth query and cuts the cross-layer reads per token from quadratic to linear in depth.

The fixed window keeps 89% to 94% of ALF's loss reduction over the standard residual and stays ahead of FBT at every scale. Its gap to learned attention grows with depth, from 0.0013 nats on MoE-1.6B to 0.0024 nats on MoE-4.0B, so learned attention earns a growing share of the gain as models deepen.
